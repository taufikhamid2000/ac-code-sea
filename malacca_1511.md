# The Fall of Malacca, 1511: What Non-Portuguese Sources Tell Us

## TL;DR
- The Portuguese narrative (Albuquerque's *Commentários*, Tomé Pires's *Suma Oriental*, João de Barros's *Décadas*) dominates the record, but Malay, Chinese, Arabic, Persian and Turkish sources independently confirm the core facts while sharply diverging on duration, causation and tone — and modern historians treat the Portuguese chronicles as reliable on hard military detail but self-glorifying and unreliable on motive, scale and the supposed "ease" of victory.
- Malacca was genuinely well-armed — per João de Barros, Albuquerque "captured 3,000 out of 8,000 artillery… 2,000 made from brass and the rest from iron," plus war elephants and a mustered force reported at 20,000 — but its guns were mostly small-calibre, foreign-sourced (Javanese and Gujarati), and crippled by a shortage of gunpowder and trained gunners; the Malay *Sejarah Melayu* itself records the Malays asking in astonishment "what is this round weapon that is sharp enough to kill?"
- Malacca was a Ming tributary that did appeal to China; the *Ming Shilu*/*Mingshi* record the Emperor ordering Siam and neighbours to help (none did), and Chinese hostility to the Portuguese over the seizure lasted three decades — but China sent no military aid in 1511 itself.

## Key Findings

**1. The duration is contested, and the Portuguese "weeks" timeline is the better-documented one.** Portuguese sources give a precise, short campaign: Albuquerque's fleet reached Malacca at the start of July 1511, a first assault failed on St James's Day, 25 July, a second succeeded on 15 August, and the city was secured around 24 August — frequently summarised as "40 days of fighting." The Malay *Sejarah Melayu* (Sulalat al-Salatin) compresses the whole event into a great assault "on the first day" and the fall of the city "on the second" — a two-day battle. These are less contradictory than they appear: modern scholarship (the Medieval Indonesia/Sulalat philological work) judges the Malay account to telescope the protracted July–August campaign into its climactic episode, and notes the *Sejarah Melayu* misremembers the 1509 first contact and the 1511 conquest as more separated in time than they actually were. The real fighting was a matter of weeks, not months, though the Portuguese remained until November consolidating. There is no credible non-Portuguese source suggesting a longer siege.

**2. Malay sources: betrayal, disunity, and a moralising frame.** The *Sejarah Melayu* attributes the fall less to Portuguese prowess than to internal moral collapse — above all Sultan Mahmud Shah's unjust execution of the powerful Bendahara Tun Mutahir (Tun Muzahir) and his family on the slander of the Laksamana Khoja Hassan/Husain and the merchant Raja Mandaliar. The Annals describe Mahmud abdicating to his son Sultan Ahmad Shah between 1509 and 1511, then having Ahmad killed after the defeat. (Portuguese sources, notably Brás de Albuquerque, instead say the Malaccan commander Ahmad fell on the field of battle — a direct, unresolved discrepancy.) The chronicle frames the catastrophe as divine retribution on a tyrannical ruler, consistent with its overall theme of *daulat* (sovereignty) and the ruler–subject bond (Cheah Boon Kheng has read it precisely as "moral judgement" on Sultan Mahmud). The *Hikayat Hang Tuah*, by contrast, is a late-17th/early-18th-century Johor epic that is largely legendary; its famous story of a Malaccan embassy to "Rum" (the Ottomans) to buy cannon never happened and is a literary borrowing from the historical Acehnese embassies to Istanbul of the 1560s.

**3. Malacca's military capability — real but hollow.** Non-Portuguese and Portuguese sources agree Malacca possessed substantial artillery. Per João de Barros's *Décadas da Ásia* (drawing on the *Commentários do grande Afonso de Albuquerque*, Lisbon 1576), Albuquerque "captured 3,000 out of 8,000 artillery. Among those, 2,000 were made from brass and the rest from iron… of such excellent workmanship that it could not be excelled, even in Portugal." Castanheda reduces the captured number to about 2,000 and reports the bridge — the decisive battleground — defended by 72 guns. Albuquerque reportedly rated Malaccan gun-founders as the equal of Germany's. But the weapons were overwhelmingly small-calibre swivel guns (the *lela*, *rentaka*, *cetbang* type), were outranged by Portuguese ordnance, and — critically — were operated by foreigners: Duarte Barbosa says the arquebus-maker of Malacca was Javanese, and Anthony Reid argues the Javanese handled most of the productive/military-technical work. The captured Portuguese factor Rui de Araújo reported to Albuquerque that the Sultan could muster 20,000 men (including Turkish and Persian bowmen) and 20 war elephants, but that the artillery was "crude and lacked enough gunners," and that only about 4,000 men were battle-ready; Araújo (who also reported the city had 10,000 homes) further recorded that Malacca lacked gunpowder, and that the Portuguese captives were pressured — unsuccessfully — to manufacture it, none of them knowing how. The *Sejarah Melayu*'s own account of the Malays' shock at cannon fire ("what is this sound, like thunder?... some lost their necks, some their arms, some their thighs... what is this round weapon that is sharp enough to kill?") — corroborated by Gaspar Correia's *Lendas da Índia* and Faria y Sousa's *Ásia Portuguesa* — strongly implies Malacca was unfamiliar with the *battlefield* use of guns in 1509, even if it had stockpiled them defensively or ceremonially. The city also had no stone walls — only bamboo/wood stockades and a fortified royal compound — which historians (Reid; Subrahmanyam & Parker) see as a decisive structural weakness.

**4. Chinese sources: a tributary that appealed, but received no rescue.** Malacca was a Ming tributary from 1405, its first ruler Parameswara having visited the Yongle court in 1411 with Zheng He, and tribute missions continued until the 1520s — contrary to the common assumption that ties lapsed after Zheng He's last voyage (Cheah Boon Kheng, *JMBRAS* 2012). The *Ming Shilu* and *Mingshi* record that after the conquest "Sultan Mamat [Mahmud] ran away and sent envoys to inform the imperial government of this disaster," and that the Emperor "issued a decree upbraiding the Franks [Fo-lang-ji], told them to go back to their own country and ordered the kings of Siam and other countries to assist their neighbour in his need; none of these obeyed, however, and so the kingdom of Melaka was destroyed." A 1521 memorial by the Investigating Censor Qiu Daolong demanded the Portuguese return Melaka before any embassy be received: "only after they have returned the territory of Melaka will they be allowed to come to Court to offer a gift." The consequence was real but indirect: the Tomé Pires embassy (1516/1517, reaching Beijing 1520) was detained and members imprisoned, tortured and executed, and Sino-Portuguese clashes followed at Tunmen (1521) and Sincouwaan/Xicaowan (1522). But China dispatched no fleet to relieve Malacca in 1511; the Ming "protection" had always been largely rhetorical (Pardesi; Cheah).

**5. Arabic, Persian and Turkish sources: the conquest "reverberated round the Muslim world."** This is the most under-used body of evidence, surveyed by A.C.S. Peacock (Bishop Wardlaw Professor of Islamic History, University of St Andrews) in "Melaka in the Arabic, Persian and Turkish sources," *Indonesia and the Malay World* 52, no. 153 (2024), who writes that the Portuguese conquest "reverberated around the Muslim world, finding echoes in a Deccani geography, an Ottoman intelligence report composed in Jeddah, and a verse chronicle in distant Anatolia." Key points:
- A **1519 Persian petition** by a senior Melaka finance official (possibly the Jew Khoja 'Izz al-Din), edited by Jorge dos Santos Alves & Nader Nasiri-Moghaddam, "Une lettre en persan de 1519 sur la situation à Malacca," *Archipel* 75 (2008): 145–166, gives a near-contemporary non-European account of the conquest's aftermath. Addressed to the Portuguese, it recounts that the inhabitants fled, that Albuquerque rebuilt and repopulated the town by offering merchants tax exemptions, and that after his departure for India constant fighting with the Malays resumed; Sultan Mahmud, ensconced at Bintan, sent expensive tribute for a peace treaty while subsisting on little but sago; governance worsened under governor Jorge de Brito (1515), whose men knew no Malay and local law, confiscated merchants' ships, and blockaded Javanese rice shipments. It is, in Peacock's words, "one of the most important documents for the history of Melaka."
- The Ottoman admiral **Selman Reis's 1525 intelligence report**, written at Jeddah (Topkapı E.6455), lists Melaka among the Portuguese-held ports and urges their destruction — but erroneously states the Portuguese "captured the port of Melaka opposite Sumatra **from Hindu infidels**," revealing how little the central Islamic lands grasped Melaka's Muslim identity.
- The Anatolian poet **Firaki Abdurrahman Çelebi's verse chronicle** (*Seʿâdetnâme*, c. 1527) devotes a chapter ("on the situation of the Franks who have gone Below the Winds") to the Portuguese conquest of Melaka and the building of the fort, drawing on a now-lost Persian pilot-guide (*Rahmani*) — proof that further, undiscovered sources once existed.
- The famous Arab navigator **Ahmad b. Majid**, writing just before the conquest, described Melaka in strikingly unflattering terms in his *al-Maʿlaqiyya* (transl. Tibbetts 1979): "The infidel marries Muslim women while the Muslim takes pagans to wife. You do not know whether they are Muslim or not. They are thieves… The Muslim eats dogs for meat for there are no food laws. They drink wine in the markets…" — which helps explain the wider Muslim-world perception (and Selman Reis's "Hindu" error). Repeated later Acehnese embassies to Istanbul — per Alves (2015), "the Acehnese embassy to Istanbul of 1562 aimed to secure support for an attack on the Portuguese in Melaka… the embassy of 1565 had the same purpose" — sought Ottoman help to retake Melaka (the historical kernel behind the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* "Rum" legend), but Ottoman intervention never materialised.

**6. Reliability of the Portuguese narrative.** Modern scholarship treats Albuquerque's *Commentários* (compiled by his son Brás, first published 1557), Pires's *Suma Oriental* (written 1512–15, lost until a Paris manuscript surfaced in 1944), Barros, Castanheda, Correia and Damião de Góis as indispensable but partisan. They are detailed and often internally cross-checkable on military matters, yet disagree among themselves — e.g. six bronze lions (Brás) versus two cast-iron lions (Góis) taken from the palace; 2,000 versus 3,000 versus 8,000 guns; population 30,000 (Góis), ~40,000 (modern estimate), or 100,000 (Albuquerque). They systematically frame the conquest as a providential Christian victory over Islam, inflate Malaccan numbers to magnify the achievement, and minimise Portuguese vulnerability. Pires himself is considered an unusually careful observer who cross-checked merchant informants, which lends his economic and ethnographic data particular weight.

## Details

**Sequence of events (reconciling the sources).** Diogo Lopes de Sequeira reached Malacca in 1509 and was initially welcomed by Sultan Mahmud, but the Gujarati and Javanese (and, per some accounts, Tamil Muslim) merchant communities persuaded the Sultan and Bendahara to seize the Portuguese; about 20 men were captured (including Rui de Araújo, who became a key intelligence source). Albuquerque returned in force in 1511 (c. 1,200–1,500 Portuguese plus several hundred Indian/Malabari and other auxiliaries, 17–18 ships). After negotiations over the captives and a demand to build a fortress failed, fighting centred on the bridge over the Malacca River dividing Upeh (merchant quarter) from Hilir (palace and mosque). The first assault (25 July) was repulsed; the second (mid-August), using an armed junk run up to the bridge plus amphibious landings, broke the defence. Mahmud, who reportedly fought from a war elephant and was wounded, withdrew up-river and ultimately to Bintan, then Kampar (Sumatra), where he died c. 1528. His sons founded the Johor and Perak sultanates.

**Why the artillery failed.** The consensus picture: Malacca had quantity but not quality or competence in gunpowder warfare. The guns were largely small swivel pieces sourced from Java, Gujarat, Pegu and India (plus one large gun from the king of Calicut); gunpowder was scarce; the Javanese specialists who operated them were too few to make the mass of guns effective; and the defenders' failure to use cannon to destroy the grounded Portuguese junk over several weeks "indicated their lack of skill with ordnance." Scholar Wan Mohd Dasuki Wan Hasbullah argues there is no evidence guns, cannon or gunpowder were manufactured in the Malay states themselves, nor that the Sultanate used guns in battle before 1511 — even on the testimony of Malay sources. Subsequent events reinforce the point that the broader archipelago, especially Java, was the real gun-power: the Demak (Java) admiral Pati Unus attacked Portuguese Malacca with a Javanese fleet "with much artillery made in Java" in 1512–13.

**The missing and destroyed record.** No indigenous Malaccan administrative archive from the sultanate survives. The *Sejarah Melayu* survives only in later manuscripts (the oldest, in St Petersburg, dates c. 1798; the 1612 Johor recension associated with Bendahara Tun Sri Lanang is the textual backbone), and there is still no complete critical edition. Peacock notes the conquest produced a sharp cultural rupture, visible in the sudden post-1511 abandonment of Arabic Qur'anic funerary epigraphy in the peninsula. The richest near-contemporary non-Portuguese documents are therefore the Chinese court records, the 1519 Persian petition, and the Arabic/Ottoman texts — each fragmentary. (Note that the later "lost archives of Melaka" debated by scholars such as Fernando and Gallop refer to the Dutch/British colonial-era records held in the British Library, not to sultanate-era documents.)

## Recommendations
- **For a balanced account, triangulate rather than choose.** Use the Portuguese chronicles for the military/chronological skeleton (dates, the bridge battle, captured-ordnance counts), but cross-check every quantitative claim against their internal disagreements and discount their providential framing. Treat "40 days" as a Portuguese summary and the *Sejarah Melayu*'s "two days" as a telescoped folk-memory of the same weeks-long campaign — not evidence of a different timeline.
- **Foreground three under-used primary sources** a general reader rarely sees: the *Ming Shilu*/*Mingshi* passages on the appeal to China and the (ignored) order to Siam; the 1519 Persian petition (Alves & Nasiri-Moghaddam 2008) for the conquest's immediate aftermath and administration; and Peacock (2024) for the Arabic/Persian/Turkish reception.
- **Flag the legends explicitly.** Treat the *Hikayat Hang Tuah* "Rum/Ottoman cannon" embassy and the *Mendam Berahi* super-galley as later Johor-era literature, not 1511 history; treat Hang Li Po (the "Ming princess," absent from Ming records) and even Hang Tuah's historicity as contested (Khoo Kay Kim found no historical evidence for him, though the Ryukyu *Rekidai Hōan* letters of 1480–81 may reference the Laksamana).
- **Benchmarks that would change the picture:** discovery/translation of additional pre-1511 Malay administrative documents; a full critical edition of the *Sejarah Melayu*; or new Ottoman/Persian archival finds (such as Firaki's lost *Rahmani* source) would materially shift the balance away from the Portuguese-dominated narrative.

## Caveats
- All the headline quantitative figures (8,000 vs 3,000 vs 2,000 guns; 20,000 men; 20 elephants; population 30,000–100,000) derive ultimately from Portuguese reporting (de Barros, Albuquerque, and Rui de Araújo via Albuquerque) and should be read as estimates by the victors. The number of guns *employed* in the fighting was far smaller than the totals reported captured.
- The *Sejarah Melayu* and *Hikayat Hang Tuah* are literary-moral and genealogical works composed or redacted generations after 1511, not eyewitness chronicles; they are invaluable for Malay perspective and causation but unreliable for chronology and numbers.
- The Chinese records are court-centred and rhetorical; Ming "protection" of Malacca was largely ceremonial, and the *Mingshi* is a much later compilation.
- Several vivid operational details (the wounded Sultan on his elephant, the armed junk at the bridge, the "lack of skill with ordnance") come from Portuguese accounts and modern reconstructions of them; they are plausible and internally consistent but not independently corroborated by non-European sources.
- The 1519 Persian petition's authorship (Khoja 'Izz al-Din) is a scholarly identification, not certain, and the petition is partisan — its author was lobbying the Portuguese for personal exoneration.
- Where this report relies on encyclopaedic aggregators, they consolidate the cited primary chroniclers; the underlying authorities (de Barros, Correia, Castanheda, Pires, the *Ming Shilu*, Cheah, Reid, Wan Mohd Dasuki, and Peacock) are the actual sources.
